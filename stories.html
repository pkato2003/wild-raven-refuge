Raven · Admitted 4 Nov · Released 19 Mar

Juniper

A raven that shouldn’t have survived, and then did.

A jogger on the Fern Ridge path called us on a Tuesday morning about a raven sitting in the gravel near the shooting range, not moving. When Priya got there, the bird was upright but couldn’t stand. It would push itself up, hold for a few seconds, and fold.

The presentation looked like spinal trauma. It wasn’t. Her blood lead level came back at 74 µg/dL — well into the range where birds are usually euthanised on welfare grounds.

We chelated her anyway. Eleven weeks, two courses, daily subcutaneous fluids, and syringe-feeding for the first three weeks. She regained her feet at day nineteen. She started flying at day forty-one. She spent another five weeks in the flight pen getting strong enough to be worth releasing.

She went back out on March 19th, about 300 metres from where she was found.

Crows · 6 weeks in care

The three from the windstorm

A February windstorm took down a 90-foot Douglas fir on a property in Coburg. The tree had an active crow nest about two-thirds of the way up. The homeowner heard the chicks before she saw them.

Three nestlings, roughly nine days old, no feather coverage to speak of. They needed feeding every ninety minutes between 6am and 10pm for the first eighteen days. Three volunteers rotated shifts. All three fledged, all three were released together in April, and two of them were still visiting the original garden in July.

Outcome: all three released

Crow · 7 weeks in care

Ash

Hit by a car on a residential street in the Whiteaker. Left wing fractured at the humerus. Devon drove him to Springfield the same afternoon; Dr. Okonkwo pinned the wing the following morning.

Four weeks of restricted movement, then two weeks of slow re-flight. What made this case worth writing down is that his family never left. Two adults and a sibling were still using the same block the entire time. We released him into that block and he was moving with them by evening.

Outcome: released back to family group

Steller’s jay · 9 days in care

The one that got away

A juvenile jay brought in after a window strike. No fractures, no bleeding, normal bloodwork. He was alert and reactive within four hours. We kept him overnight for observation and released him the next morning from the same garden.

Most window strikes aren’t this lucky. This one was, because the homeowner called us within ten minutes and did exactly what we told her to do, which was nothing but put it in a box.

Outcome: released at 24 hours

Raven · 22 days in care

Nine Mile

An adult raven picked up near the Nine Mile marker on Highway 126, unable to fly. Radiographs showed an old, healed fracture in the right wing — at least two years old. This bird had been flying on a bad wing for years and had finally lost the strength to compensate.

There was nothing to fix. What he needed was conditioning. Twenty-two days in the long pen, gradually rebuilding, and he was flying well enough to release. Banded and released at the same location.

Outcome: released after conditioning

Crow · 3 days in care

The cat bite

A crow found in a back garden in Bethel, unable to use its right leg. Two puncture wounds on the thigh, small enough to miss. The finder waited three days before calling, because the bird “seemed to be getting better.”

By the time she reached us, the infection had reached the bone. She was euthanised the same evening.

Outcome: euthanised on welfare grounds

Crow · 1 day in care

The fledgling that didn’t need us

A healthy crow fledgling, fully feathered, hopping on the ground in a front garden. Its parents were in the tree above it, screaming at the caller. This is completely normal behaviour — crow chicks leave the nest before they can fly, and spend about a week on the ground while their parents feed them.

We talked the caller through it and she agreed to leave the bird alone and keep her dog inside for a week. It flew off nine days later.

Outcome: never admitted. Best possible result.

Magpie · 5 weeks in care

Pica

A magpie admitted after being found on the ground, thin and weak. Radiographs showed eleven small metal objects in the gizzard. She had been eating things she shouldn’t have, probably from a construction site or a burned-out lot.

Two of the objects passed naturally. Three were removed surgically. The rest we managed with a bulk diet and careful monitoring. She regained weight steadily and was released at five weeks.

Outcome: released

Crows · ongoing

The roost at Alton Baker

Every winter, several thousand crows roost in the trees at Alton Baker Park. Every winter, we get a handful of calls about birds found on the ground underneath, usually juveniles that got pushed off a perch in the night.

Most of them are fine — cold, hungry, and disoriented, but not injured. We assess them, warm them, feed them for a day, and release them back into the roost at dusk. It’s the least dramatic work we do, and one of the most satisfying.

Outcome: 14 of 17 released within 48 hours

Raven · 4 days in care

Gunshot

An adult raven brought in after being found in a field near Junction City. Radiographs showed a single lead pellet in the body cavity and a fractured coracoid. The fracture was not survivable. She was euthanised.

We include this one because it’s the case that made us start talking publicly about lead. One pellet. One bird. Multiply that by every field and every range in the valley.

Outcome: euthanised on welfare grounds
