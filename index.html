Who we are

A small refuge with a very narrow focus.

We’re not a zoo, and we’re not a general wildlife hospital. We’re a handful of licensed rehabbers and volunteers who specialise in corvids — the birds most people walk right past.

Ravens, crows, magpies, jays. Animals that mourn their dead, hold grudges for years, recognise individual human faces, and pass that knowledge on to their chicks. They are, by any measure, some of the smartest creatures in this valley. They are also the ones most likely to end up on the shoulder of a road.

Most of what comes through our door is preventable. A car strike. A cat. A window. A chick that left the nest a week too early. A bird that ate something it shouldn’t have, in a place it shouldn’t have been.

Our permits let us do the work. They don’t pay for it. That part is on us, and on people like you.

What we do

From the phone call to the release.

Three stages, and a lot of sleepless nights in between.

Rescue & transport

The phone rings at 6am and again at 11pm. We talk the caller through catching and boxing the bird safely, then someone drives out to collect it. Most of our intakes come from within 40 miles of Eugene.

Treatment & recovery

Wound care, splinting, fluid therapy, lead chelation and a lot of hand-feeding. We work with an avian vet in Springfield for X-rays and surgery. The rest happens here, in the flight pens out back.

Release & follow-up

A bird only goes back when it can fly hard, forage, and recognise a threat. Where we can, we release into the same territory it came from — and into the same family group, if it still has one.

Rescue stories

Three that made it back out.

Not every case ends well. These three did.

Raven · 11 weeks

Juniper

Found on a gravel shoulder near a shooting range in November, unable to stand. Bloodwork came back with lead levels high enough to kill her. Eleven weeks of chelation therapy, syringe feeding and slow re-flight training later, she went back out in March. Same road, different outcome.

Crows · 6 weeks

The three from the windstorm

A February storm took down a Douglas fir with an active nest in it. Three chicks, maybe nine days old, no feathers to speak of. Fed every ninety minutes from six in the morning until ten at night for three weeks. All three flew.

Crow · 7 weeks

Ash

Hit by a car on a residential street. Fractured left wing, pinned for four weeks. What kept us hopeful was that his family never left. Two adults and a sibling were still in the same block when we released him, and they took him back the same afternoon.
