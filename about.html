About us

We started with one crow
in a laundry basket.

That was 2019. We’ve since taken in over eleven hundred birds, and we still keep the laundry basket.

How it started

One bird, one bad decision, and a permit application.

In October 2019, Marion Kestrel found a juvenile crow on the sidewalk outside a Fred Meyer in south Eugene. It had a broken leg and a lot of opinions. Every wildlife hospital in the area was either full, closed, or four hours away.

She took it home in a laundry basket. She did almost everything wrong that first night — gave it water, tried to splint the leg herself, kept it too warm. The crow survived anyway, mostly out of spite, and was released eleven weeks later in the same parking lot it came from.

What stuck with her wasn’t the bird. It was the phone calls. Over the next six months, word got around that someone in Eugene would actually pick up. She took in fourteen more birds that winter, applied for a state rehabilitation permit in the spring, and filed for 501(c)(3) status with three other people who kept showing up to help.

We’ve been over capacity ever since.

Holding a bird correctly is the first skill we teach. It takes about four hours to learn and a lifetime to get right.

What we believe

Four rules we don’t break.

01

The bird comes first.

Not the donor, not the photo opportunity, not the volunteer’s feelings. If handling a bird for a better picture risks its recovery, we don’t take the picture.

02

Wild means wild.

We don’t imprint, we don’t hand-tame, we don’t keep “ambassador birds.” Every corvid that comes through here is a candidate for release until a vet says otherwise.

03

Honest numbers.

We publish how many birds we admit, how many we release, and how many we lose. A rescue that only posts the happy endings is selling you something.

04

Prevention is cheaper.

Half our budget goes to treating things that were entirely avoidable. So we spend real time telling people how to avoid them.

Licensing

What we’re legally allowed to do.

Wildlife rehabilitation in Oregon is tightly regulated, for good reason. If someone is taking in native birds without a permit, they’re committing a federal offence — and they’re usually doing more harm than good.

  • USFWS Migratory Bird Rehabilitation Permit — MB-048219, active through 2027
  • Oregon Department of Fish & Wildlife — Wildlife Rehabilitation Permit 0482-R
  • State of Oregon nonprofit registration — Registry No. 1842931-90
  • IRS 501(c)(3) — EIN 84-2910337, effective March 2020

We’re happy to send copies of any of these to anyone who asks. We’d rather you check than assume.
