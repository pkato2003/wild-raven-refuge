Our work & services

What actually happens
to a bird that comes in.

Every admit follows the same six steps. Depending on the injury, it takes anywhere from four days to five months.

01Intake & triage 02Stabilisation 03Diagnostics 04Treatment 05Flight conditioning 06Release

Step 01

Intake & triage

The bird arrives in a box. Before it comes out of the box, we already know roughly what we’re dealing with.

The person who found it has already told us the location, the circumstances, and how long it’s been down. That information matters as much as anything the bird will tell us later — a bird from a busy road is a different case from one found in a quiet garden.

We weigh it first, always. Weight tells us whether we’re dealing with an adult in good condition, a starving juvenile, or a bird that’s been down for days. Then a quick physical: eyes, beak, wings, legs, keel, vent, and the inside of the mouth.

  • Species, age class, and sex (where we can tell)
  • Body weight and keel score
  • Obvious fractures, wounds, or bleeding
  • Neurological signs — head tilt, circling, poor grip
  • Any sign of having been caught by a cat

Cat bites are the single most urgent intake we see. The puncture wounds are tiny and the infection is catastrophic. A bird with a cat bite that reaches us within 12 hours has a good chance. At 48 hours, it usually doesn’t.

Step 02

Stabilisation

Nothing else happens until the bird is warm, hydrated, and out of shock.

A bird in shock cannot be treated, cannot be X-rayed, and cannot be operated on. Its body is prioritising survival over everything else, and adding stress at this stage kills more birds than the original injury does.

So we do almost nothing. Dark box. Quiet room. 85°F. Warm fluids subcutaneously. Then we wait, sometimes as long as 24 hours, before touching it again.

This is the part volunteers find hardest. Everything in you wants to do something. The correct clinical answer is usually to leave the bird alone.

85°F

Target temperature for a shocky corvid

6–24 hr

Typical stabilisation window before diagnostics

Step 03

Diagnostics

Once the bird is stable, we find out what’s actually wrong.

Devon drives our surgical cases to Springfield, where Dr. Okonkwo takes digital radiographs and does the procedures we can’t do here. For everything else, we run in-house bloodwork.

The single most useful test we run is a blood lead level. Corvids are scavengers, and the Willamette Valley has a lot of spent ammunition in the soil. Lead poisoning presents as weakness, head tilt, or an inability to stand — and it looks identical to a spinal injury until you test for it.

Radiographs

Fractures, swallowed objects, lead fragments, gunshot.

Blood lead level

Anything above 20 µg/dL gets chelation. Above 60 is critical.

Fecal & oral swabs

Trichomoniasis, coccidia, and other things that spread fast in captivity.

Ophthalmoscope exam

Eye trauma is common in window and vehicle strikes, and easy to miss.

Step 04

Treatment & hand-rearing

Two very different jobs under one roof: fixing injured adults, and raising orphaned chicks.

Injured and sick adults

Wound management, surgical pinning for fractures, chelation therapy for lead, and a long course of antibiotics for anything that came out of a cat’s mouth. Most adult cases spend two to six weeks in the clinic and then move to the flight pens.

Orphaned chicks

Hand-rearing a corvid is a full-time job with a very short deadline. A nine-day-old crow needs feeding every ninety minutes, sixteen hours a day. The formula has to be the right temperature. The chick has to be the right temperature. Get either wrong and you lose it in a night.

We also have to be careful not to let the chicks imprint on us. Feeding is done with a puppet, handling is minimised, and as soon as they can feed themselves they go in with other juveniles.

Daily feeding window for a nestling

90 min

Interval between feeds at peak

Step 05

Flight conditioning

Surviving an injury is not the same as being fit to live wild.

A bird can be clinically healed and still be unable to survive. A wing that mended slightly short. A bird that lost condition during six weeks in a box. A juvenile that never learned to forage because it grew up in a pen.

Our flight pens are 60 feet long and 18 feet high. Birds go in once they can fly, and they stay until they can fly hard — sustained, banking, landing accurately, and reacting to a threat. That takes weeks. Sometimes months.

Juveniles go in together, in groups, so they can learn from each other. We scatter feed and hide it under bark and in trays so they have to work for it. They get live prey items where appropriate. Nothing is handed to them.

  • Sustained level flight for 100+ feet without tiring
  • Accurate landing on a moving or narrow perch
  • Foraging: finding hidden and scattered food
  • Appropriate fear response to a human and to a hawk silhouette
  • Normal weight and pectoral muscle mass for the species

Step 06

Release

Back to the same road, the same block, the same family — whenever we can manage it.

Corvids are territorial and intensely social. A crow released into a strange neighbourhood is an intruder, and resident birds will drive it out or kill it. So we release where the bird came from, at the same time of day, and watch.

When a bird still has family in the area, we wait for them. Ash’s parents were still in the same block four weeks after he was hit. We opened the carrier and he was gone into the trees within about ninety seconds, and by evening he was moving with them again.

We follow up where we can. Some birds are banded. Most aren’t — leg bands on corvids are hard to read and hard to justify on a bird that’s already been through enough. We rely on the people who called us in the first place to tell us what they see.

When release isn’t possible

Some birds can’t go back. A wing that won’t fly, an eye that’s gone, neurological damage from lead. For those, we work with licensed educational facilities and sanctuaries. We never keep a non-releasable bird ourselves, and we never place one somewhere we haven’t visited.

Beyond the clinic

What else we do.

24-hour rescue line

A real person answers, or calls you back within ten minutes. We walk callers through safe containment even when we can’t take the bird ourselves.

Referral & advice

We keep a current list of every licensed rehabber in western Oregon and will send it to anyone who asks. No charge, no membership.

School & community talks

We visit classrooms, garden clubs and neighbourhood associations around Lane County. Free, and we bring photographs, not live birds.

Every one of these steps costs money.

X-rays, chelation drugs, formula, fuel, heat lamps. It adds up fast.
